01 — The short version

A full battery is a small, daily, avoidable waste.

Rooftop solar often produces more electricity than a house can use in the middle of the day. By early afternoon a home battery is already full, the panels keep working, and the extra power goes back to the grid for almost nothing. Meanwhile the house two doors down is buying its electricity at full price.

The problem
Surplus has nowhere to go. Once storage is full, generation becomes waste — and the household that could use it has no way to ask for it.
The idea
Give it to a neighbour, not to the grid. No money, no trading, no middleman — a street that treats its shared solar as a shared resource.
The object
Not an app. A thing on the wall. It lives in the hallway where the whole household walks past it, and it can be read from three metres away.

02 — Why this exists

Energy consumption in residential areas is not optimised in Australia.

At the end of 2025 about 39% of dwellings in New South Wales had rooftop solar. Generation peaks at midday, when the house is empty and the car is at work; a home battery fills by mid-afternoon. After that the inverter caps output and the surplus is exported for a feed-in tariff worth a fraction of the retail price paid that evening.

Batteries are arriving fast — 43,788 were installed in NSW in the five months after the federal rebate began. But a battery does not remove the problem, it only delays the moment when a house has nowhere left to put the power.

03 — Choosing a direction

Three concepts, put in front of users before anything was designed.

Each concept was written up, explained in a short interview, and scored against six criteria — usefulness, sustainability, social value, feasibility, learnability and fit with daily routine. The point was not to pick the cleverest idea, but the one that survived contact with real people.

Concept 01

Virtual energy marketplace

A peer-to-peer exchange where households sell surplus electricity to each other and earn credits or cash.

Killed for: transactional to the core. People liked the money and remembered nothing else — no environmental or social reason to keep using it.

Chosen
Concept 02

SunLink Neighbours

Surplus power is given to neighbours for free, and you can ask for power back when you need it. A street-level network rather than a market.

Kept because: it was the only concept that scored on sustainability and on community at the same time. Its weakness — it depends on goodwill — became the design problem.

Concept 03

Smart home integration

A hub that talks to every appliance in the house, scheduling loads around generation so that nothing is wasted.

Killed for: technically the strongest, socially the weakest. It optimises one house and ignores the street — and it competes in a crowded market.

04 — The logic under the interface

One rule decides everything: how much charge is left.

The entire product hangs on three states. Colour is not styling here — it is permission. A green battery means you may give power away. A blue one means you may neither give nor ask. A red one is the only state in which you may ask someone else. Switch between them below.

100%12 kWh / 12 kWh
Full · you can give

The house on the wall turns green and the storage panel fills. This is the only state in which sharing is possible, so it is the only state that shows the sharing panel as active.

  • Requests arrive here. A neighbour asking for power pops up on the community map with Accept and Ignore.
  • Auto-Share appears. Turn it on before a trip and the system answers requests for you.
  • Sharing is never accidental. Accepting opens a second confirmation naming the neighbour.
72%8.6 kWh / 12 kWh
Balanced · you can do neither

Between 21% and 99% the device goes quiet. You cannot give power away and you cannot ask for it either — the system protects your own evening use first. Most of the day looks like this.

  • No pop-ups. Neighbour requests are suppressed; the community map keeps updating as a background picture.
  • Only four things still respond: Auto-Share, Information, Generation Efficiency and Data.
  • The sharing panel says why: “You could not share power or send request” — a refusal with a reason, not a dead button.
16%1.8 kWh / 12 kWh
Insufficient · you can ask

Below 20% the battery turns red — the same red that tells a neighbour you need help. Here the interface inverts: the sharing panel becomes a way to ask the street rather than to answer it.

  • One action: “Send a power request?” — sent to the households currently marked green.
  • Receiving is drawn as a line. An amber line appears from the neighbour to your battery, so direction is never ambiguous.
  • It stops on its own at 80%, so you cannot accidentally drain a neighbour.
Share only at 100% Nothing leaves your battery until it is completely full. Being generous is never allowed to cost you the power you already have.
Ask only below 20% Under 20% the button changes direction: you are the one asking the street for help, and only houses marked green can answer.
Ceiling at 80% Sharing stops automatically once the receiving house reaches 80%, before it becomes dependent on you.
Auto-Share For a five-day trip: turn it on and requests from the street are accepted while you are away, with nobody at home.

05 — Four seconds, once a day

What actually happens, from the wall to the neighbour's meter.

The everyday interaction is almost nothing: the device says the battery is full, someone asks, you confirm. Everything else is background. These four screens are the whole of it.

Step 01

The battery hits 100%

The house on the wall glows green and the cable from the roof lights up. From this second on, surplus is spare.

Step 02

Amanda asks

Her request appears at the bottom of the community map with her name, her photo and two buttons. Nothing else on the screen changes.

Step 03

You confirm

Accept never shares immediately. A second sheet names the person — the fix for the single most repeated finding in testing.

Step 04

The line lights up

You and Amanda are drawn at either end of a lit line, 2 kW moving between you, and a Stop Sharing button that is always there.

07 — Why it is a device and not a feature

Sharing power is a switch, not a button.

Each house has three parts: a solar panel, a battery and an inverter. One single-pole double-throw switch decides where the current goes. Not sharing — the current charges your battery. Sharing — it routes to the inverter and out into the street. There is no software trick behind it; the interface is only a remote control for a physical decision. Try the switch.

Parallel, not serial Every house sits on the same pair of shared lines. One household can drop out without taking the street down.
One switch, two futures Charge yourself, or feed the street. The model forces that choice to be visible instead of hiding it inside an app.
Why build it at all A screen cannot explain a circuit. A physical model can be picked up, wired and pointed at during a conversation.

08 — The street you can turn around

Six houses, six cables, and one hub in the middle.

09 — It existed outside the screen

We built the street twice: once in cardboard, once in plastic.

The first street was six paper houses on a cardboard board, wired with red and green tape — red for the parallel circuit, green for the two houses actually exchanging power. It was fast, cheap and good enough to test whether the idea made sense at all. The second was the printed version, made once the concept had earned the effort.

10 — Five rounds

The useful part was not the version that worked.

Five prototypes, four rounds of testing, and one score that went down. The details below are the ones that changed the design, not the ones that flattered it.

Lo-fi 1.0Phone app

“Unclear how the community map works” — nobody in the first round could tell what the map was for. The tutor added the bigger note: a phone is the wrong device for a home control centre, and it has no presence in the house at all.

Keep the features, throw away the device

Lo-fi 2.0Wall display

Rebuilt as a screen that hangs on a wall, with four buttons that feel like they belong there. The red low-power state was understood instantly — colour worked before anything else did. People also asked to compare their usage against the street average.

Add live data graphs and a community reference line

Mid-fiCardboard circuit

Six participants, and the first real number: SUS 74. The failures were specific — nobody discovered that day / week / month was a slider, and accepting a request gave no confirmation whatsoever.

Make the periods visible tabs; add a confirmation step

SUS 74
Hi-fi 1.0One crowded page

A modelled house replaced the illustration and the score went down: SUS 66.6 across eight participants. Button text was too small, the house took half the screen, and nobody could tell what green, blue and red meant without being told.

Split the page in two and label the states

SUS 66.6 ↓
Hi-fi 2.0The final build

Model page and Control Panel page became two places, and the information button explains one board at a time. Three more users then found the last two ambiguities: the minus sign made direction unclear, and it was still hard to see who was sharing with whom.

Redraw the connecting lines; onboard one board at a time

11 — Known issues & future versions

What this build cannot do yet.

This was a course prototype: the interface and the physical model were built and tested, but the service behind them — accounts, servers, inverters talking to each other — was never built. So the honest list of what would break first is short, and none of it is cosmetic. These are the four the team wrote down in the final document, and what a later version would have to do about each.

01

Security

There is no real authentication. Anyone who reaches the device can read a household's consumption history, or answer a request as that household.

Next version: role-based access, multi-factor sign-in for the owner, a separate installer role, and encryption for anything in transit or stored.

02

Connectivity

Sharing depends on a live connection. A weak or insecure network stalls the handshake, and a request that arrives late is a request that was never answered.

Next version: a signal check at installation, a repeater where the wall is far from the router, and a local fallback so the battery still reads correctly offline.

03

Hardware compatibility

Panels, batteries and inverters come from different manufacturers. Anything outside the standards we tested against simply would not appear on the screen.

Next version: a published compatibility list, testing against the common inverter protocols, and a clear installation guide instead of a support ticket.

04

Accessibility

The interface assumes sight, touch precision and colour recognition — three assumptions that exclude people the street would otherwise include.

Next version: state labels in words as well as colour, larger touch targets, a screen-reader pass, and testing with people who use assistive technology.

Future versions

From one street to any street

The version we tested only works because every house runs the same hardware. The longer-term direction is interoperability: a layer that keeps the three rules — give at 100%, ask below 20%, stop at 80% — while the equipment underneath changes.

  • Adaptive frameworks, so every function survives different devices and systems
  • Coverage of the common battery and inverter protocols
  • A local fallback for when the connection drops
  • Accessibility testing with people who use assistive technology
