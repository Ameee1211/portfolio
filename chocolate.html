01 — The short version

A Flower You Could Eat
Made in a Mould

Four colours, four flavours — each one cast in a mould pulled from a 3D print. The interesting part is not the casting: it is that the flower was drawn three times before one version could be moulded at all. I also made the packaging and packed it, so the whole making process runs from the model to the box.

SketchThree shapes before one was printable
PrintTwo masters — 15mm petals, then 4mm
MouldOne vacuum-formed sheet, two cavities — only one was better suited to chocolate
FinishFour flowers, trimmed by hand, packaged into a flower shape

02 — Four shapes

Every version was an answer to the one before it.

The first was a chocolate bar printed with the polka dots. Safe, and forgettable — a bar with a pattern on it is still a bar. The second was a flower, but only a symbol of one: flat petals, no depth, nothing to bite into. The third was drawn from a real rose, with petals that overlapped and curled the way they do in life. It was the closest to the thing, and the one the mould could not take. So there was a fourth.

Four answers, one thread: each version was redrawn around what the last one could not do. The fourth is not a compromise — the angled outer petals make it the best-looking of the four, and it is the one that comes out of the mould.

03 — Two prints

Same printer. Two prints.

Both masters were printed at 0.15mm layers on an Ultimaker 2, a couple of hours each.

04 — Under the hood

Every petal needed its own fillet value.

Rounded edges read as a flower petal rather than a gear tooth, but a generous fillet makes Fusion refuse to build the edge at all. Each petal was filleted separately, checked, and adjusted when it failed.

Every petal wall leans outward by 10°. A vertical wall would grip the set chocolate, and it would break as it came out.

05 — How I made the mould

Plastic stretches. It does not fold into a slot.

The mould is made by clamping a sheet of plastic over the printed master, heating it until it sags, then pulling a vacuum underneath. The sheet is dragged down over the model and takes its shape. Where the surface is broad and shallow, it follows. Where a petal drops into a narrow gap, it bridges straight across the top.

On version three the petals were 15mm deep. The sheet closed over the gaps instead of entering them, and the mould came out with the outline of a flower and none of its depth. Version four cut the petals back to their top curve — 4mm at the rim, a −10° draft on every one so it could release. That is the one that fills.

06 — Making it

How I handmade the chocolate using the mould

I cast both versions to compare them. Version three, on the right, has the deeper petals, and 15mm is more than the sheet can follow: it bridged the gaps instead of entering them, the chocolate filled those bridges, and the flower came out as a dark disc with its petals flattened into the surface.

Version four, on the left, was cut back to 4mm — and its outer petals sit on an angle, which is what makes it read as a small open flower rather than a solid block. Shallow is not a compromise here: every petal edge survives the sheet, the chocolate keeps them, and this is the mould the final batch was made from.

07 — Four flavours

Four flavours, one shape.

One flavour would have been enough to prove the mould works — and nothing else. Four turn it into something you choose from instead of something you are handed. Chocolate, strawberry, blueberry, lavender: the same flower cast four ways, so there is a piece for whoever is opening the box rather than one piece they have to like.

08 — The box

The chocolate is three-dimensional, so the box had to be too.

A flat carton would have flattened the whole idea. The box is folded from one die-cut sheet into an eight-pointed star, and when the points close it reads as a paper flower — the same object as the chocolate inside, in a different material.

The pattern is the polka dots again, in the four colours of the flavours, so the box says what is inside before you open it. The dieline was the only part that had to be exact: a millimetre off and the points miss each other.

09 — What I would change

Two things I would do differently.

01

Vacuum forming eats the fine detail

Pulling a sheet over the master only holds the outline. Layer lines and small surface texture get stretched away, so the chocolate comes out with the shape of the flower but not the grain of one. A platinum-cure silicone mould — the master buried in silicone, cured, then cut open — would have kept that detail, and would not have needed the petals flattened at all: the depth limit only exists because this mould is a sheet.

02

The box has no inner tray

The star holds the chocolate on a table and looks right doing it, but nothing fixes the piece in place. Tilt it and the chocolate slides into a point. One folded platform and an extra score line would have solved it without adding material.
