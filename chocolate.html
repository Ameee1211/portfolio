A flower drawn from a real rose could not be moulded: the petals were too deep for a vacuum-formed sheet to follow. So the shape was rebuilt from the mould's side — shallower petals, a draft angle on every one — and what came out was a chocolate you could turn over in your hand.

01 — The short version

A flower you could eat, made in a mould that refused to hold a flower.

The brief asked for a gift. The gift became a chocolate flower — four colours, four flavours, each one cast in a mould pulled from a 3D print. The interesting part is not the casting. It is that the flower was drawn three times before one version could be moulded at all.

SketchThree shapes before one was printable
PrintTwo masters — 15mm petals, then 4mm
MouldOne vacuum-formed sheet, two cavities
FinishFour flavours, trimmed by hand, folded into a star

02 — Three shapes

Every version was an answer to the one before it.

The first was a chocolate bar printed with the polka dots from the story. Safe, and forgettable — a bar with a pattern on it is still a bar. The second was a flower, but a symbol of one: flat petals, no depth, nothing to bite into. The third was drawn from a real rose, with petals that overlapped and curled the way they do in life.

Both screenshots are the same file, a week apart. The difference between them is not a taste decision. It is what the mould could follow.

03 — The mould said no

Plastic stretches. It does not fold into a slot.

The mould is made by clamping a sheet of plastic over the printed master, heating it until it sags, then pulling a vacuum underneath. The sheet is dragged down over the model and takes its shape. Where the surface is broad and shallow, it follows. Where a petal drops into a narrow gap, it bridges straight across the top.

On version one the petals were 15mm deep. The sheet closed over the gaps instead of entering them, and the mould came out with the outline of a flower and none of its depth. Version two cut the petals back to their top curve — 4mm at the rim, a −10° draft on every one so it could release. The second version is not prettier. It is the one that fills.

04 — Two prints

Same printer, same file, two hours apart.

Both masters were printed at 0.15mm layers on an Ultimaker 2, a couple of hours each. The only thing that changed between them is the depth of the petals — and whether the sheet can get in.

05 — Making it

Eight steps, and one that decides the other seven.

Slice in Cura. Print the master. Vacuum-form the mould. Melt the chocolate. Colour and flavour it. Pour, then tap the mould until the bubbles come up. Chill. Trim the flash off the rim with a knife. The last step is the one that makes it look like something you would give away rather than something you made.

06 — Four colours

Four flavours, one shape.

Brown is chocolate. Pink is strawberry. The blue one is blueberry — white chocolate plus blue colouring, which reads closer to mint than to blue. Purple is lavender: the only one where the shape and the flavour are the same thing. All four come from the same place as the dots on the box.

The four sit on the same plate as a silver leaf — the only prop in the photographs, and the only thing on the table that is not edible.

07 — Under the hood

Every petal needed its own fillet value.

Rounded edges read as a flower petal rather than a gear tooth, but a generous fillet makes Fusion refuse to build the edge at all. Each petal was filleted separately, checked, and adjusted when it failed.

The draft angle is the quiet one. Without it the chocolate grips the petal walls and tears on the way out. With it, the piece releases with a knock.

08 — The box

The chocolate is three-dimensional, so the box had to be too.

A flat carton would have flattened the whole idea. The box is folded from one die-cut sheet into an eight-pointed star, and when the points close it reads as a paper flower — the same object as the chocolate inside, in a different material.

The pattern is the polka dots again, in the four colours of the flavours, so the box says what is inside before you open it. The dieline was the only part that had to be exact: a millimetre off and the points miss each other.

09 — What I would change

Two things I would do differently.

01

Vacuum forming eats the fine detail

Pulling a sheet over the master only holds the outline. Layer lines and small surface texture get stretched away, so the chocolate comes out with the shape of the flower but not the grain of one. A platinum-cure silicone mould — the master buried in silicone, cured, then cut open — would have kept that detail, and would not have needed the petals flattened at all: the depth limit only exists because this mould is a sheet.

02

The box has no inner tray

The star holds the chocolate on a table and looks right doing it, but nothing fixes the piece in place. Tilt it and the chocolate slides into a point. One folded platform and an extra score line would have solved it without adding material.
